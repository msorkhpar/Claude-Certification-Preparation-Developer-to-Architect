Level: Architect · Module 78: Exam readiness 3 · Page 2 of 5 Exams: A1 to A5 (CCAR-F)

After this page you can use the Level 3 flashcards and the spaced-review question bank on a schedule, read a mock exam result by domain, by scenario and by cause of the miss, and run a four-week plan that spends time where the marks are.

Written from the course's own revision files and the exam map, on 2026-10-04. The spaced-review schedule is the one module 11 introduced; the plan is this course's working method and the guides do not publish a method for preparing. The official exam guide and the Anthropic pages decide every fact about the exam, so check them before you rely on a number here.

Why it matters

Level 3 holds more than sixty pages of design decisions, and nobody keeps all of it in mind. The mock exams tell you how you stand, and the revision files tell you how to move. Without a routine, revision becomes rereading the pages that felt good the first time. With one, each session targets a named weakness, returns to it on a schedule, and is finished when the weakness stops appearing in the mock.

Spaced review

Memory fades quickly and recovers with well-timed practice. The schedule is the same as in modules 11 and 44 and is stored in the bank as intervals_days: review a new card or question on the day you meet it, then after 1, 3, 7, 14 and 30 days, each counted from the previous review. An item that you answer correctly moves to the next interval, and an item that you miss goes back to the first. Three habits make it work.

  • Answer before you look. Say the answer aloud, or write it, then turn the card. The effort of recall is what strengthens memory, and a look at the answer is not recall.
  • Keep sessions short and daily. Twenty minutes every day beats three hours on a Sunday.
  • Read the page behind a miss. A wrong answer on a card or an item names a page. Reread that section once, then retest it tomorrow.

Reading a mock result

A percentage hides what you need. Score by domain and by scenario, using the tables on the mock page, and keep an error log with one line for every miss. Write down three things: the question's number and domain, the page that the key names, and the cause.

CauseWhat it looks likeWhat to do
Did not knowYou have no memory of the factReread the page, make a card for the fact, add it to the schedule
MisreadYou knew it and answered another questionRead the constraint first; slow down on "first", "best" and "most likely"
Took a distractorYou knew the topic and chose a plausible mistakeRead the key's reasons for each wrong option, and name the mistake in your log

The distribution of causes tells you what to change. Mostly "did not know" in one domain is a study problem. Mostly "misread" everywhere is a technique problem, and no more reading of pages will fix it. A cluster of misses in one scenario, with no pattern in the domains, means that the setting itself slows you down: reread its capstone module (70 to 75) and work its example again. The mock exam is not a conversion to the exam's score: the pass mark is a scaled score of 720 on a scale from 100 to 1,000, and no percentage is published for it. Use the figures to decide where to work, and not to predict the result.

A four-week plan

The plan assumes about forty minutes on most days and a little more at the weekend. Change the numbers to your time, and keep the order.

  1. Week 1: baseline. Take mock exam 1 untimed, with no notes, and read every explanation. Fill the error log. Rank the domains by the weight of the domain times the share you missed. Start the flashcard schedule for the top two domains.
  2. Weeks 2 and 3: the weak domains, by weight. Work the review bank for the ranked domains, and reread the pages behind your misses. Make a card for every fact that you did not know. In the second week add the next domain on the list. Keep the daily flashcard session going; it is short, and it is the part that holds.
  3. Week 4: exam conditions. Take mock exam 2 in one sitting of 120 minutes, with no notes and the pacing of page 1. Compare its domain scores with the first mock. Spend the next three days on the domains that still lag. The mock exams use different questions on purpose: the second is a check on what you learned, and not a repeat of the first.
  4. The last two days. Review flashcards and your error log only, and check the logistics of module 11, page 2: the name on the booking against the ID, the booking time, the system check or the journey.

If your first mock already shows an even profile, shorten the plan and move mock exam 2 earlier. If it shows a gap in a heavy domain, add a week and begin with that domain's pages, not its cards: cards recall a fact, and a pattern needs the page.

What to do the day before

Stop studying new material. Read the error log once, the flashcards that were missed twice and the decision patterns on page 1. Sleep. The mock exams are practice, and one more of them on the last night teaches you little and costs you rest.

Traps

  1. Rereading what you already know. The pages that felt easy are the ones you reread. Work the misses.
  2. Memorising the mock. The questions are the course's own and will not appear in the exam. What carries over is the reasoning, so learn why each wrong option fails.
  3. Treating "misread" as bad luck. A misread is a repeatable habit. Name it in the log and fix the habit.
  4. Skipping the schedule. A card reviewed once and never again is forgotten. The intervals are the method.

Cards

Cards written for this site from several pages of this level. Nothing here is graded, and what you mark is kept in this browser.

  1. Front

    What ends a run of the agentic loop in the SDK, and what does a reply that holds a tool request mean?

    Back

    The model's own stop value ends a normal run. A reply that holds a tool request is not the end, whatever its text says: run the tools and continue.

  2. Front

    What is a turn ceiling for, and how should a run that reaches it be reported?

    Back

    It is a backstop against runaway loops, set roomy so it rarely fires. A run that stops at it has its own outcome, a status that is not done.

  3. Front

    If two tool calls depend on each other, how should the loop handle them?

    Back

    Put them in two turns. The model decides in one turn what to ask for, and the SDK promises no order between calls made together.

  4. Front

    Does the usage figure on a result cover the whole tree of subagents?

    Back

    No. It counts one loop only. The documentation names a separate field for the whole tree, so use that for cost accounting.

  5. Front

    A run keeps failing to converge and ends with a stuck status. Where do you look first?

    Back

    At the saved conversation. The status signals a run that does not converge, and the conversation shows why, often a helper's weak description or error message.

  6. Front

    What does a session that crashed return in place of a normal result?

    Back

    An error result of the kind error_during_execution. Its cost fields may be zeroed and its stop reason may be null, so a pipeline must not trust its figures.

  7. Front

    Why must a coordinator put what it knows into a subagent's prompt?

    Back

    The subagent's context starts fresh. The brief is the only channel for facts the coordinator already found, such as paths, messages and earlier decisions.

  8. Front

    When is one single conversation better than a team of subagents?

    Back

    When the phases depend on one shared context and on steering, which a team cannot give.

  9. Front

    One of four research scopes fails. What does the coordinator do?

    Back

    It records that scope as failed and tells the user what is missing, since a failure belongs to its scope. The other scopes continue.

  10. Front

    How does a coordinator close gaps its review found?

    Back

    It sends follow-ups for the flagged gaps alone, within a fixed number of rounds, so the loop is bounded.

  11. Front

    Two subagents return overlapping work. Where is the cause?

    Back

    In the briefs. They did not divide the scope or say what each part must leave out, and subagents cannot see each other.

  12. Front

    How should the size of a team follow the question?

    Back

    Scale it to what is asked. A plain fact needs one agent with a few tool calls, and only a wide question justifies a larger team.

  13. Front

    The model rarely delegates to a specialist subagent. What do you change first?

    Back

    The description. The model reads it to decide whether to delegate, so it must say when the agent applies.

  14. Front

    Why match two tool names when reading subagent calls in the stream?

    Back

    Older versions named the delegating tool Task in the call blocks, and current versions use another name, so code that reads both works on either.

  15. Front

    When does delegated work run in the foreground?

    Back

    Only when the call asks for it. The documented default is a background launch, so a coordinator that needs the result first has to ask for a foreground run.

  16. Front

    How should subagents return claims so that a merge keeps every source?

    Back

    A team wants agents to read tickets but not create or delete them. What is the rule?

    Back

    Allow the get tool by name and deny the create and delete tools by name.

  176. Front

    Why must a team note list every variable the MCP servers use?

    Back

    Because every variable that appears in the project's server file has to appear in the note, so a missing one is visible.

  177. Front

    What does an audit say about a header named Authorization with a literal token value?

    Back

    It is a secret-looking name whose value has no reference, so the secret sits in a committed file. Use a variable reference instead.

  178. Front

    What makes a read-only explorer subagent definition pass?

    Back

    A description that starts with Use when, a tools line that exists and lists only read-type tools.

  179. Front

    Why is a default value for a token variable a fault?

    Back

    A default for a token puts a usable credential into a committed file.

  180. Front

    A pipeline step hangs waiting for input. What is the fix?

    Back

    Add the print flag so the run answers and exits. It is the documented way to run without a person.

  181. Front

    Two jobs: a digest nobody waits on and a gate a developer waits on. Which goes to a batch?

    Back

    Only the digest. Nobody waits for it, while the gate stays in real time.

  182. Front

    What makes a review criteria set pass an audit?

    Back

    Two items to report and two to skip, and an example for every severity, with no vague phrase such as be conservative.

  183. Front

    How is a multi-pass review of a large change structured?

    Back

    A pass for each file, then an integration pass that reads the file findings together.

  184. Front

    What must a CI gate do with a run that returned no valid answer?

    Back

    Fail the job, reading the exit status and the JSON, since no valid answer exists.

  185. Front

    A review job holds a shell tool but only looks at code. What does an audit say?

    Back

    The job holds a means of changing things it does not need. A review needs reading tools only.

  186. Front

    A required value is absent from the document. What does the pipeline do?

    Back

    Sends the record to a person after one attempt. No retry supplies what the page lacks.

  187. Front

    How do you report an extraction run honestly?

    Back

    Report the accuracy on everything received and on the accepted ones, with the difference stated.

  188. Front

    What happens to a forced tool choice on Opus 5.5, Sonnet 5.5 and Fable 5.1?

    Back

    The call fails with a 400 error. The documented replacement is auto with strict schemas or structured outputs.

  189. Front

    What are the batch result types other than success?

    Back

    Errored, canceled and expired. A batch expires if it does not complete within 24 hours, and each custom id is 1 to 64 characters.

  190. Front

    When may a kind of document be automated?

    Back

    When it has at least the minimum number of documents and accuracy at least the target. Both comparisons are inclusive.

  191. Front

    When does the audit call a figure overstated?

    Back

    Only when the gap between the validated and the all-document figure is greater than the policy gap. Reaching the gap is not enough.

  192. Front

    In what order are an extraction run's failure shapes fixed?

    Back

    Invented values, then wasted retries, then unchecked totals, then an overstated figure. The order follows the damage downstream.

  193. Front

    Why count unchecked totals only among accepted documents?

    Back

    A reviewed case was seen by someone, and a failed one never went out, so only an accepted record can carry an unchecked total.

  194. Front

    A message shows a sign of risk. Where does it go first?

    Back

    To a safety hand-off. A signal of risk is checked first, before any other handling, and in code.

  195. Front

    A record holds a postal address and a subscription plan marked to verify. How does the assistant treat them?

    Back

    Uses the postal address and has the subscription plan confirmed before relying on it.

  196. Front

    How does a file tool stay inside its root?

    Back

    It checks every path in every command and refuses whatever resolves outside the root.

  197. Front

    What does the assistant do on a distress message?

    Back

    Acknowledges it, says someone is coming and stops advising. Such messages are routed in code before the model sees them.

  198. Front

    Two of twenty conversations repeat a question and the limit is ten percent. Is it met?

    Back

    Yes. A limit is met at the limit, so exactly ten percent repeated questions pass and twenty percent do not.

  199. Front

    A stored address is 200 days old and the limit is thirty days. What does the assistant do first?

    Back

    Marks it by its age and has the person confirm it before relying on it. An old fact is verified before it is used.

  200. Front

    A generated tool's code fetches a page that its proposal never declared. What happens?

    Back

    It is refused, since code that does more than it declared is refused, whatever the rest looks like.

  201. Front

    What is a text scan of generated code good for?

    Back

    A cheap first check for the obvious. It is not a sandbox, so the environment holds back the rest.

  202. Front

    A tool's result does not match its schema. What happens?

    Back

    It is rejected before the agent uses it, and the audit records the rejection.

  203. Front

    A proposal has a forbidden call and a weak description. What is the decision?

    Back

    A refusal, with the weakness still noted beside it. The most serious outcome wins and the report keeps all findings.

  204. Front

    A timeout request equals the policy ceiling. Is it accepted?

    Back

    Yes. A limit is inclusive, so a value that equals it is allowed and one more is flagged.

  205. Front

    An approved tool is changed. What happens?

    Back

    It goes through the gate again as a new proposal, since the person approved what they read.

  206. Front

    Where is the audit of tool runs kept?

    Back

    One line per decision and per run, written by the gate and runner and kept where the agent has no access.
