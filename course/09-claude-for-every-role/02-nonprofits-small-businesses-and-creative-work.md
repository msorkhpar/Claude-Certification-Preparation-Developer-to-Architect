## Why it matters

Small organisations and individual makers meet the same risks as large companies with fewer safeguards: no legal
department to read the policy, no review layer, tight budgets and personal reputations at stake. The Associate exam
scenarios are often set in such places (a small team, a communications role, a consultant), and the wrong options
tend to skip a check that a large organisation would have built in. Knowing who owns what in each setting tells you
which check the person must supply themselves.

## The idea

### Recap: the loops

Page 1 of this module gave the frame: **Delegation, Description, Discernment, Diligence**. The creative-work course states
the structure neatly: Description and Discernment form an inner loop (say what you want, judge the result, say it
better), and Delegation and Diligence form an outer loop (decide what to hand over, then stand behind what you
produce). Two tests follow in every setting: *did I choose the right thing to delegate*, and *did I check enough
before I own it*?

### Non-profits

The public course, developed with a partner organisation, is for staff in fundraising, communications, programme
delivery, operations and leadership. Its lessons pair research and writing with the description and discernment loop,
and privacy, data and data analysis with the delegation and diligence loop, then turn to workflow augmentation. It
frames the setting as limited resources, several stakeholders to whom the organisation answers, and mission-driven
work.

- **Delegates:** first drafts of grant proposals and appeals, summaries of research and reports, programme-evaluation
  outlines, donor-thank-you variations, meeting notes.
- **Describes:** the funder's criteria, the organisation's mission and voice, the audience (a foundation officer, a
  donor, a community member) and the facts that must appear.
- **Checks:** every claim about impact, every statistic and every funder requirement against the source; whether
  the language fits the community described; whether a draft overstates what the programme achieved.
- **Owns:** what the organisation tells funders and the public, and the data entrusted to it by beneficiaries and donors.
  Donor and beneficiary records are sensitive; the usual rule applies: classify first, remove identifiers before
  analysis, and follow the organisation's policy and the law (module 10).

*Exam angle:* data analysis with personal records is the classic scenario; the best answer anonymises or aggregates
first and checks results, not "upload the donor list and see".

### Small businesses

The public course was developed with several small and mid-size business partners. It addresses staff in customer
interactions, back-office management, supply chain and leadership, in a setting of limited resources, direct business
accountability and customer experience. Its topics include the framework, what AI can and cannot do, transparent AI
use and refining with AI.

- **Delegates:** customer-reply drafts, product descriptions, invoice-reminder wording, job-ad drafts, summaries
  of supplier terms, a first pass at an inventory or sales analysis.
- **Describes:** the customer, the tone of the brand, the policy that applies (refund windows, delivery times) and
  what must never be promised.
- **Checks:** prices, dates and commitments in anything a customer will rely on; that an answer matches the actual
  policy and not a plausible one; and that a confident reply to a customer is not invented.
- **Owns:** the promise made to the customer and the business's legal position. A small owner has no one else to
  catch the error, so the check is built into the routine: a short list of facts to verify before sending.
- **Is open about it:** the course's "transparent AI use" topic points to telling customers and staff plainly
  where AI is involved, in line with any rule that requires it (module 5, module 10).

*Exam angle:* high-volume, low-stakes drafting suits a faster, cheaper model (module 3) with a spot check; a
contract, a legal term or a customer's money does not go out on trust.

### Creative work

The public course for creative professionals (writers, illustrators, designers, musicians and other makers) begins
from a different question: whether, where and how AI belongs in a creative practice. It teaches two analytical
frameworks, a **creative value lens** and a **production lens**, and recommends a deliberate starting point of no AI,
adding it only where analysis says it earns its place. It names authorship, distinctiveness and the relationship with
an audience as the considerations particular to the field, and treats AI "roles" as outcomes of the delegation
decision.

- **Delegates:** the parts of the production that do not carry the maker's voice: transcription, resizing and
  formatting, variants for different platforms, research summaries, brainstorming angles, first outlines.
- **Keeps:** the part that makes the work theirs: the idea, the voice, the final choices and the signature.
- **Describes:** references, constraints, the audience and what must stay in the maker's own style.
- **Checks:** originality (does it echo someone's existing work?), accuracy of any factual claim, tone, and whether the
  piece still sounds like the maker.
- **Owns:** authorship and the audience's trust. Disclosure matters more here than in many fields: audiences,
  commissioners and platforms may have rules or expectations about AI involvement, and the maker answers for them.

*Exam angle:* in a scenario where the person's value is their distinctive voice, the right answer protects it
(delegate production, keep authorship) and is honest about the AI's part.

### How the shared ideas shift

| | Non-profit | Small business | Creative practice |
|---|---|---|---|
| Delegation weighs on | Drafting and summarising, with data kept out | High-volume, low-stakes drafting | Production tasks, never the voice |
| The check that matters most | Impact claims and statistics against source | Prices, dates and promises against policy | Originality and accuracy |
| Diligence is mainly about | Beneficiary and donor data | The customer promise | Authorship and disclosure |
| Hidden risk | Overstating results to a funder | No second reviewer | Sounding like everyone else |

### A rule that holds in every role

Ask three questions before any AI-assisted work leaves your hands: *Who is relying on this? What would hurt them if it
were wrong? What did I check, and what do I need to say about how it was made?* The answers set the amount of checking,
and they differ by role only because the people relying differ.

## Traps

1. **Uploading beneficiary or customer records "to see what it finds".** Classify first and remove identifiers; module
   10 gives the classes.
2. **Letting a fast draft stand in for a commitment.** A customer reply that states a price or a deadline is a promise
   the business owns; check it before it is sent.
3. **Delegating the voice.** For a maker the distinctive voice is the product; delegate production and keep authorship.

## Quiz

1. A non-profit's analyst must show a funder where demand is highest, using a sheet of
   beneficiaries' names, addresses and health notes. Policy restricts sharing personal data. Which approach fits?
   - **a**: Summarise it to district counts, then verify those counts
   - **b**: Paste ten full records first to test the method on a sample
   - **c**: Switch to a faster, cheaper model for the sensitive records
   - **d**: Upload the full sheet and ask Claude to leave names out of its findings

2. A small shop owner has Claude draft replies to customers about delivery times. One reply promises delivery in two
   days, a figure the owner never supplied. What is the best response?
   - **a**: Stop using Claude for customer contact altogether, starting today
   - **b**: Send it, as the draft sounded confident and professional enough
   - **c**: Ask Claude whether the figure is right, and send if it agrees
   - **d**: Check each date and commitment against the actual policy

3. An illustrator wants to use Claude on a picture-book project and cares about being recognised for their style. Which use
   fits best?
   - **a**: Ask it to generate the whole story in their style, then sign it
   - **b**: Hand it production tasks, and keep the ideas and final choices
   - **c**: Have it write the narrative and present the result as unaided work
   - **d**: Let it decide the visual direction, since it knows many styles

## Module quiz

This quiz covers both pages of the module.

1. Two staff members each receive a readable draft from Claude: a lesson rubric for one, a funding appeal for the other. Which pair of checks matches what each of them owns?
   - **a**: First: reading level and layout; second: tone and brand voice
   - **b**: First: pupils' reactions once it is handed out; second: donors' reactions once it is sent
   - **c**: First: accuracy and outcome fit; second: impact claims against sources
   - **d**: First: Claude's own rating of its accuracy; second: Claude's own rating of its sources

2. A freelancer working for a charity wants Claude to write personalised thank-you notes that cite each supporter's
   past gifts, working from the full supporter list. Charity policy says personal data may not leave its systems
   unprotected. What should happen first?
   - **a**: Send twenty complete records to test the tone of the notes
   - **b**: Strip names and addresses, then use templates with merge fields
   - **c**: Pick a faster, cheaper model, since thanks are low-stakes drafting
   - **d**: Ask Claude to confirm that it will not keep the supporters' details

3. A student's course bans AI help on a graded essay. A freelance illustrator's commission contract is silent on AI.
   Both are tempted to use Claude. What fits each of them?
   - **a**: The first leaves it out; the second uses it for production and says what was done
   - **b**: The first uses it for planning only; the second uses it and says so
   - **c**: The first leaves it out; the second uses it and says nothing unless a client asks
   - **d**: The first leaves it out; the second uses it freely and stays silent, as the contract says nothing

4. Claude turns a wholesaler's data sheet into allergen notes for a bakery's new product line. One note says "may
   contain nuts" for an item that the sheet marks nut-free. What should the owner do?
   - **a**: Treat it as a one-off slip and correct only that item
   - **b**: Ask Claude to explain how it reached that note, then trust the rest
   - **c**: Check every statement against its source before anything goes out
   - **d**: Add a line saying an AI drafted it, and publish the rest unchanged
